The Orchestrator and the Plan

You manage the orchestrator. The orchestrator manages the work.

Its job

The orchestrator is one agent per line of work. You tell it what you want, whenever it comes to you. It turns that into tasks on a board, decides what comes first, starts other agents on the work, and asks you when only you can decide. It never edits code itself: when you ask it to do something, it puts the work on the board and says who will do it.

It writes everything down as it goes, on the board and in the plan. A new session, or a restart, picks up from what’s written rather than from memory.

The charter

The charter is the orchestrator’s rules for this line of work, in your words: how work lands, what done means, who reviews, what it may decide alone. It’s written from a short interview the first time you start an orchestrator, and it rereads the charter each time it picks the work back up, so editing it changes how work is done from then on.

The plan

The plan is what you read first. It sits beside the orchestrator’s conversation and answers, from top to bottom:

  • Needs You: what’s waiting on you.
  • Landing, In Review and Building: what’s moving now, and how far along it is.
  • Waiting: what starts next, in order.
  • Agents: who’s working, and what it cost.
  • Themes: why each group of tasks exists, and how much of it is done.
  • Decided For You: calls the orchestrator made on your behalf, for you to keep or reverse.

An empty plan offers Suggest Themes: the orchestrator reads recent commits, pull requests and issues, suggests a few outcomes to work toward, and you choose which to keep.

Talking to it about one task

Every task has Ask the Orchestrator, which starts a message naming that task in the orchestrator’s box, so you can say what you want changed without describing which task you mean.
